\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This is an audit, not a causal memo. For each state law and San Francisco ordinance that
plausibly bears on the project it asks what the law changed, what in the data would move if it
worked as written, whether the treated set can be constructed, and what would confound a
comparison. It reports no effect.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{\rtLaws\ laws, every date and scope claim quoted from a primary source.} The inventory
holds \rtCa\ California statutes and code sections and \rtSf\ San Francisco ordinances; its
\rtClaims\ claims all verify against leginfo, the Board's ordinance lists and the enacted PDFs.
One row, the 25-unit ordinance of July 2026 named in the brief, is unverified: none of the
\rtSfTwentySix\ ordinances the Board lists for 2026, through Ordinance \rtSfLastOrd\ (effective
\rtSfLastEff), matches it.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{\rtUsable\ laws generate usable variation; \rtNotMeasurable\ are not measurable here.} The
usable ones are SB~330's preliminary-application freeze and hearing cap, the inclusionary
changes of Proposition~C, Ordinance~158-17 and Ordinance~201-23 (measured through the exaction
panel), and the Housing Production ordinances (effective \rtEffHpTwoZeroTwoThree\ and
\rtEffHpTwoZeroTwoFour) that removed hearings. Every opt-in pathway --- SB~35, SB~423, AB~2011,
the density bonus, HOME-SF --- is a developer's choice bundled with labour and affordability
conditions, and the permits memo found \pcMinisterialPermits\ such permits.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{The docket's composition moved after those ordinances.} Residential conditional-use
items fell from \rtResCuPre\ a quarter in \rtWinPre\ to \rtResCuPost\ in \rtWinPost, and residential
discretionary reviews from \rtResDrPre\ to \rtResDrPost. Every series measured on the docket since
then is measured on a different sample; the memos that use them need an era split there.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{SB~330's five-hearing cap does not show in the hearing counts.} Of residential cases
first heard in \rtHearWinPre, \rtOverFivePre\% were heard on more than five dates; of those
first heard in \rtHearWinPost, \rtOverFivePost\%, before the later cases have finished.
\end{abstract}

\section{What this memo is, and is not}\label{sec:rtintro}

A referee will ask why the paper does not use SB~35, or SB~330, or the Housing Production
ordinances. This memo is where the answer comes from. For each law it gives four things: what
the law changed, in the law's own words; the observable that must move if the law was
implemented at all (the mechanical first stage) and the one that moves only if people responded
(the behavioural second stage); whether the projects the law reached can be identified in the
data; and what else changed at the same time. It ends with a verdict per law and a shortlist.

It does not estimate anything. Where a series appears to move, the memo says so and says what
would have to be true for the movement to be the law's doing. No event-study coefficient,
difference-in-differences or synthetic control appears here.

\section{The model objects, and which rows to care about}\label{sec:rtcats}

Each law is assigned to one primary category by the model object it moves, with secondary
effects noted:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Arrival of discretion} --- whether a project faces a hearing at all (ministerial
pathways, removal of conditional-use requirements): \rtCatOne\ laws.
\item \textbf{The delay distribution given discretion} --- statutory timelines, hearing caps,
CEQA constraints: \rtCatTwo.
\item \textbf{Stringency in dollars} --- inclusionary requirements, fees: \rtCatThree.
\item \textbf{The option's term and vesting} --- the preliminary-application freeze, when fees
vest: \rtCatFour.
\item \textbf{The feasible envelope} --- density bonuses, upzoning, parking minimums, ADUs:
\rtCatFive.
\end{enumerate}
Categories~1 and 2 are the potential identifying variation for the wedge: they change $\sigma$
or whether it applies, not the envelope. Category~5 laws are controls. Category~3 is $\tau$ and
is measured in the exactions memo.

\section{Sources and how they were checked}\label{sec:rtsources}

Each law's facts are claims in \texttt{law\_claims.csv}: the field, the value, the source URL
and a quotation taken from the source's cached text by a regular expression; dates are read out
of the quotation. \texttt{probe} re-reads every source; all \rtClaimsOk\ of \rtClaims\ claims
verify. California statutes are quoted from leginfo's bill text (chapter, approval date, the
Legislative Counsel's digest, sunset clauses) and code sections; San Francisco ordinances from
the enacted PDFs and the Board's lists.

\paragraph{Effective dates.} A San Francisco ordinance's effective date is the Board's list.
For a California statute the Constitution sets the rule --- ``go into effect on January 1 next
following a 90-day period from the date of enactment of the statute'' --- and this memo quotes it
but does not compute from it, because what counts as the date of enactment is not sourced here.
Instead, where a code section the bill added or amended still carries the bill's own history
note, that note's date is used (\rtEffNotes\ statutes). In every such note the date is the
January~1 after the year of the Governor's approval, including for SB~79, approved on
\rtEnactedSbSevenNine\ and effective \rtEffSbSevenNine; the other \rtEffInferred\ statutes are given that date and marked as inferred
($^i$ in Table~\ref{tab:rtinventory}). A bill that says it takes effect immediately takes effect
on approval.

\paragraph{Where the Board's record is now.} The Board's own ordinance lists moved from
sfbos.org to sf.gov during 2026; the archived copy stops at Ordinance~52-26. Legistar's web API
for San Francisco carries no matter after 2020. The 2026 list used here is sf.gov's.

\paragraph{A correction to the brief.} The brief lists AB~1763 among the Housing Accountability
Act amendments. Its title is ``Planning and zoning: density bonuses: affordable housing''; it is
filed here under the density bonus (category~5).

\section{Summary and shortlist}\label{sec:rtsummary}

\rttable{rtsummary}

\paragraph{The shortlist.} The laws that come out usable are: \rtShortlist. SB~330 identifies
two things --- a vesting change (the preliminary application fixes the standards and fees) and a
cap on hearings --- both with a constructible first stage. The inclusionary changes are the
cleanest stringency variation in the project and are measured, with their vesting rules, in the
exactions memo. The Housing Production ordinances removed conditional-use and notice
requirements for specified housing projects; their first stage is the docket itself
(Section~\ref{sec:rtdiversion}), and the treated set needs the Priority Equity Geographies
polygon joined to parcels, which is the next step.

\paragraph{What the opt-in pathways cannot do.} SB~35, SB~423, AB~2011, SB~4, the density bonus
and HOME-SF are elections. A project that uses one has chosen it, and has accepted the
affordability and labour conditions that come with it; the choice is the outcome, not the
assignment. The data can count the elections --- \rtPopSbThirtyFive; \rtPopSbFourTwoThree;
\rtPopDbl; \rtPopHomesf --- and the permits memo found \pcMinisterialPermits\ ministerial permits
in DBI. They are descriptive material.

\section{The raw series}\label{sec:rtseries}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_first_stages.pdf}
\caption{First stages, per quarter where the data counts them: faint, quarterly; bold,
four-quarter mean; dotted lines, the laws' effective or operative dates. Records and permits run
to the last complete quarter before retrieval. The last panel counts, for residential cases, the
share heard on more than five distinct dates, by the year of the first hearing; recent years are
right-censored.}\label{fig:rtfirst}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:rtfirst} plots the first stages the data can see. They are descriptions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SB~35 and SB~423.} Records naming either pathway are few until SB~423 and rise
after it; \rtPopSbThirtyFive. That the rise follows SB~423 is consistent with the
extension and its changes mattering, and equally with the Housing Element rezoning of the same
period.
\item \textbf{SB~330.} Preliminary applications appear after its effective date
(\rtEffSbThreeThreeZero, inferred), rise, and fall back; \rtPopSbThreeThirty. The hearing counts do not fall: \rtOverFiveNPre\ of \rtCasesPre\ residential
cases first heard in \rtHearWinPre\ were heard more than five times, and \rtOverFiveNPost\ of
\rtCasesPost\ first heard in \rtHearWinPost. For the cap to be visible the count would have to be of
the hearings SB~330 counts, on the projects it covers (those with a preliminary application),
and neither is established; continuances are hearing dates here.
\item \textbf{ADUs.} DBI's ADU permits rise before the state package and fall after it;
\rtPopAdu. The state statutes do not produce a visible step against the local program already
running.
\item \textbf{Density bonus and HOME-SF.} Density-bonus mentions rise after HOME-SF's
adoption (\rtEffHomesf) and stay; HOME-SF's own rise and then fall away.
\end{itemize}

\section{The diversion series}\label{sec:rtdiversion}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_diversion.pdf}
\caption{(a) The Commission's docket by request type, per quarter, \rtFirstQ--\rtLastQ, with
residential items (items whose description mentions dwelling units, housing or residential use)
as a line. (b) Shares of conditional use, discretionary review and large project
authorisations, and the share of development-relevant DBI permits that are Commission-linked, by
filing quarter. Dotted lines: the category-1 laws.}\label{fig:rtdiversion}
\end{figure}

If a law diverts projects out of the discretionary track, the docket that remains changes
character, and every time series measured on it --- in every memo --- is measured on a shifting
sample. Figure~\ref{fig:rtdiversion} is that check.

The docket averaged \rtItemsPre\ items a quarter in \rtWinPre\ and \rtItemsPost\ in \rtWinPost.
Residential conditional uses went from \rtResCuPre\ to \rtResCuPost\ a quarter and residential
discretionary reviews from \rtResDrPre\ to \rtResDrPost. The break follows Ordinance~248-23
(effective \rtEffHpTwoZeroTwoThree) and Ordinance~53-24 (\rtEffHpTwoZeroTwoFour), which exempted
specified housing projects from Section~311 notice and from the conditional-use requirement; it
also coincides with the interest-rate cycle and the Housing Element rezoning. The permit-side share of
development-relevant permits that are Commission-linked was \rtLinkedEarly\% in \rtLinkWinEarly\ and
\rtLinkedLate\% in \rtLinkWinLate.

The practical consequence: analyses on the hearing docket should be split at the Housing
Production ordinances, and a comparison of hearing outcomes across that date compares different
projects.

\section{The 25-unit ordinance of July 2026}\label{sec:rtjuly}

The permits memo drew a line at 25 units for ``the July 2026 ordinance'', a figure from the brief
that memo did not check. It is checked here. The Board lists \rtSfTwentySix\ ordinances for
2026, through Ordinance~\rtSfLastOrd\ (effective \rtSfLastEff); \rtSfMatch\ of their titles
mention the inclusionary program or a 25-unit threshold. The ordinances touching housing in 2026
are a transit-oriented alternative plan under SB~79 (Ordinance~82-26), special use districts, a
tenant-protection ordinance and a definitions ordinance. Unless the ordinance meant is not an
ordinance (a Commission resolution or a Planning Department bulletin), or is not yet on the
list, the 25-unit line has no source; the row stays in the inventory, unverified. The 25-unit
tier that the inclusionary program has had since Proposition~C (exactions memo) is a candidate for what
was meant.

\section{Established and not established}\label{sec:rtest}

\paragraph{Established.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The inventory: \rtLaws\ laws with chapters, approval dates, sunsets and the laws' own
statements of what they do, every claim verified against a primary source.
\item The classification: \rtCatOne\ laws move the arrival of discretion, \rtCatTwo\ the delay
given discretion; \rtOptIn\ are opt-in pathways.
\item The docket's residential conditional-use and discretionary-review items fell after the
Housing Production ordinances.
\item No 2026 ordinance on the Board's list, through Ordinance~\rtSfLastOrd, matches a 25-unit
ordinance of July 2026.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Not established.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The effective dates of \rtEffInferred\ statutes (inferred from the pattern of the history
notes, not read).
\item Whether any law caused the movements the series show; this memo does not ask.
\item The treated sets of the opt-in pathways, and eligibility under any of them.
\item Whether SB~330's hearing cap binds: the hearings it counts are not identified.
\item Which Priority Equity Geographies parcels the Housing Production exemptions excluded (the polygon is
not yet joined).
\end{enumerate}
